# Preface

Many organizations are already working on OSS use and risk management.
Licenses, vulnerabilities, supply chains — these are important, and addressing them is correct. Yet many organizations stop there.
Meanwhile, some organizations publish and contribute to OSS, exert influence over its development direction, secure leadership positions in the ecosystem, and earn lasting recognition.

What separates organizations that stall at OSS use and risk management from those that achieve leadership positions in the ecosystem?
It is not a difference in technical capability. It is not a difference in budget. It is whether they possess a **"structure"** that generates competitive advantage.

This gap does not show up in quarterly or annual results.
But viewed over five or ten years, who defines technology standards and who stands at the center of the ecosystem — that gap becomes an irreversible difference.
This connects to macro challenges at the national level, including securing digital sovereignty and addressing the digital trade deficit.

> The true value of open source is not technology.
> It is not activity.
> It is not culture.
>
> It is the **"structure"** that connects to the outside,
> transforms that connection into value,
> and develops sustainably alongside the ecosystem.

This book is an attempt to articulate that "structure" and describe it as something that can be designed.
Every organization that uses OSS can build that structure.
The only question is whether they design and implement it.

# Chapter 1: Why OSS Doesn't Change Organizations

## 1.1 OSS and Open Source Are Different Things

This book uses the following terms distinctly.

* **OSS**: The software itself
* **Open source**: The activity, culture, community, ecosystem, and structure behind it

Most organizations use OSS.
But their connection to open source cannot be called sufficient.

> OSS use ≠ Open source engagement

Recognizing this difference is the starting point for everything.

---

## 1.2 Using It, Yet Falling Behind

> Most core systems are built on OSS.
> Cloud, data, and AI foundations rest on OSS.
> Nearly every application contains OSS in abundance.
> There is not a day in development that OSS is not used.

And yet, organizational competitiveness has not changed dramatically.

Why?

**Because they are only using it.**

---

## 1.3 OSS as a Commodity

OSS use is no longer a differentiating factor.

* Anyone can use it
* It can be adopted at low cost
* The same technology stacks are widely shared

Therefore, using OSS itself cannot generate competitive advantage.

It is simply standing at the starting line of competition.

---

## 1.4 What Has Changed Is the Premise of Competition

In the past, wielding acquired technology could itself be a competitive advantage.

That is no longer the case.

> **It is not having the technology,**
> **but being able to participate in how technology evolves, that constitutes competitive advantage.**

Most organizations have not adapted to this change.

---

## 1.5 Where Organizations Stop

Most organizations' OSS engagement stops at the following stage.

```
Use
 ↓
Connect
```

* Using OSS
* Participating in communities

They reach this point.

But they go no further.

* It doesn't connect to the business
* It doesn't accumulate in the organization
* It doesn't become competitive advantage

---

## 1.6 The Core of the Problem

The problem is not OSS.

The problem lies in the organization's own structure.

> **There is no structure to transform external connections into business outcomes.**

Without this structure, no matter how much OSS is used or how many communities are joined, the result does not change.

---

## 1.7 This Book's Position

This book does not explain how to use OSS.

Nor does it recommend open source activity.

This book has exactly one concern.

> **How to transform external connections into organizational competitive advantage**

---

## 1.8 This Book's Conclusion

The conclusion first.

> **Maintaining a state in which the organization can contribute to open source is the organization's competitive advantage.**

This is a question of organizational structure.

> And structure can be designed, implemented, and maintained.

This book will unpack where any organization currently stands and what should be designed.

# Chapter 2: Misconceptions about OSS

## 2.1 Why Misconceptions Arise

OSS has become too familiar.

* It can be used for free
* It can be adopted easily
* Many technologies are provided as OSS

As a result, OSS became "a matter of course."

And its essence and true value were left behind.

---

## 2.2 Misconception ①: OSS is free software

The most common misconception is treating OSS as free software.

It is true that OSS is often available at no cost.

But that is neither its essence nor its true value.

> **The value lies not in price, but in the structure that produces OSS.**

---

## 2.3 Misconception ②: OSS is a tool for improving development efficiency

OSS does improve development efficiency.

* It can be reused
* It has a track record
* It is supported by the community

But once that becomes the objective, the value of OSS is limited.

It is nothing more than efficiency improvement.

> **Efficiency can generate profit, but it does not necessarily increase value.**
> **It can even drag organizations into price competition, degrading value.**

---

## 2.4 Misconception ③: OSS is technology

OSS is often treated as technology.

* Technology selection
* Architecture
* Development process

But its essence is not there.

> **The essence is in the community where people who create technology gather.**

---

## 2.5 Misconception ④: Open source activity is what matters

Recommendations to engage in "open source activity" have increased.

* Participate in communities
* Contribute

But touching the essence of activity and actually generating value from it are separate questions.

> **The value of activity must be identified and connected to the organization and its business.**

---

## 2.6 The Consequence of Misconceptions

These misconceptions produce the same result.

* Use OSS
* Contribute to OSS
* Still fail to generate competitive advantage

Because:

> **They have not touched the essence or understood the true value.**

And without understanding the true value, no amount of effort can connect to results.

---

## 2.7 Conclusion

To restate this book's position clearly.

* OSS is the software itself — free and an efficiency tool.
* The essence of open source is a culture of development and the community that embodies it.
* The value of open source is found by connecting it to and transforming it into the business.
* The true value of open source is the structure that sustainably develops this essence and value.

> **What matters is understanding all of this, applying that structure to the organization,**
> **and realizing the true value.**

# Chapter 3: Finding the Value of Open Source

## 3.1 Technology Is Outside

The major technologies of today cannot be completed within a single organization.

* Cloud native
* Data infrastructure
* AI

All of these are evolving on top of open ecosystems.

Therefore, what organizations must engage with is not "the software itself."

> **It is the arena of evolution.**

---

## 3.2 Open Source Is a Structure

Open source is not simply a development methodology.

It is a structure consisting of the following elements.

* Community (people)
* Process (decision-making)
* Governance (rules)
* Ecosystem (relationships)

Within this structure, technology is continuously updated.

---

## 3.3 What Is Connection?

Connection is not merely obtaining information.

* Participating in discussions
* Understanding the background of decisions
* Standing at the frontier of problems

In other words, it is **participating in the place where change happens**.

---

## 3.4 The Depth of Connection

Connection has depth.

```
Observe
 ↓
Participate
 ↓
Influence
 ↓
Lead
```

Most organizations remain at "observe" or "participate."

But the value that contributes to competitive advantage comes from "influence" and above.

### ■ Examples

* Google released container technology standards as Kubernetes, designing the entire structure of the cloud-native market.
* Meta defined the common language of frontend development as React, drawing developers worldwide into its own technology context.
* Microsoft continues to maintain a connection with developers through VS Code.

---

## 3.5 Why Connection Is Necessary

The reason is simple.

> Technology evolution happens outside

Remaining closed internally leads to:

* Slower follow-through
* Lower quality decision-making
* Slower learning

---

## 3.6 Why Connection Alone Is Not Enough to Win

Here is an important truth.

**Connection alone does not generate competitive advantage.**

* Information is available to anyone
* Participation alone creates no differentiation

Connection is a prerequisite, and regardless of depth, it must be transformed into value.

---

## 3.7 What Lies Beyond Connection

The value of connection lies in what comes after.

* Understanding external knowledge
* Applying it to the organization's context
* Transforming it into the business

Without this transformation, connection holds no value.

---

## 3.8 Conclusion

The value of open source emerges from external connection.

But connection is only the starting point.

> Connection is potential, not value.

Value emerges only when it is transformed into meaning within the business.

# Chapter 5: The Missing Piece — The Conversion Circuit

As we have seen, most organizations do reach the point of connection.
But they stop there.

There is one reason.

> **The conversion circuit has not been designed.**
> **And until it is designed, value will never emerge.**

---

## 5.1 Overview of the Conversion Circuit

```
External Connection (Connect)
 ↓
Understanding (Context)
 ↓
Reframing (Reframe)
 ↓
Problem Solving (Solve)
 ↓
Proposal (Propose)
 ↓
Business Outcomes (Win & Scale)
```

This flow is not merely a process.
It is a **"circuit" in which roles, information, and evaluation are bound together**.

---

## 5.2 Requirements at Each Stage (What Is Needed)

### ■ Connection (Connect)

* Continuous participation in the community
* Access to Issue/PR/Design discussions
* Understanding of Foundations/Working Groups

**Output**: Raw issues, discussions, and directions

---

### ■ Understanding (Context)

* Grasping the background, constraints, and trade-offs of changes
* Understanding roadmaps and non-functional requirements

**Output**: The ability to explain why things are the way they are

---

### ■ Reframing (Reframe)

* Translation into the organization's/customer's context
  Example: "A bug fix proposal to the upstream community"
  → "An investment that distributes future maintenance costs externally (to the community)"
* Alignment with existing assets
* Redefining risks and value

**Output**: Knowledge transformed into a form usable by the organization

---

### ■ Problem Solving (Solve)

* Implementation, verification, and operational design
* Feedback to OSS (bug reports/improvement proposals)

**Output**: Reproducible solutions (code/design/runbooks)

---

### ■ Proposal (Propose)

* Constructing a narrative (problem → solution → value)
* Estimates, risk presentation, roadmap

**Output**: A value proposition that reaches the customer

---

### ■ Business Outcomes (Win & Scale)

* Winning contracts, continuity, horizontal expansion
* Knowledge reuse and standardization

**Output**: Revenue, reusable assets, brand

---

## 5.3 Role Decomposition (Who Does What)

It is difficult for one individual to carry the entire conversion circuit. Roles and division of responsibility are required.

* **Connector**: Remains embedded in the community
* **Translator**: Converts external context into internal context
* **Implementer**: Builds solutions through to completion
* **Architect**: Weaves insights into value
* **Owner**: Connects results to the business

**All of these roles are necessary.**

---

## 5.4 Information Flow (How to Flow It)

* External → Internal: Discussion logs / design intent / reasons for changes
* Internal → External: Bug reports / improvement proposals / use cases
* Internal cross-cutting: Reusable designs, templates, implementations

**Key point**: Not documentation, but **packaging in reusable units**

---

## 5.5 KPI Design (How to Evaluate)

Short-term revenue alone must not drive the circuit. Design across multiple axes.

* Number of OSS-originated projects (per month)
* Ratio of OSS-derived proposals (%)
* Reuse rate (reused outcomes / total outcomes)
* Community contribution count (PR/Issue/Review)
* Influence positions (number of Reviewers/Maintainers, etc.)

**Principles**:

* Measure not only input (connection) but also **transformation and outcomes**
* Evaluate not only individuals but also at the **team/organizational level**

---

## 5.6 Minimum Viable Model (MVP)

The conversion circuit can start small.

But what matters is **designing for scale from the beginning** — making it expandable to the entire organization.

* 1 person: Connector (syncs with external weekly)
* 1 person: Implementer (carries through from PoC to operations)
* 1 person: Architect (translates to customer value)

These 3 people run 1 theme.

This is the "minimum configuration" — **not the final form**.
Even if one theme doesn't go well, one must be prepared to continue until the structure is implemented.

---

### ■ The Premise of Scale

Competitive advantage in OSS is determined not by individuals but by **the density of involvement across the entire organization**.

* At large IT companies with leadership positions, contributors are distributed throughout the organization
* Contribution is not only from a handful of heroic engineers — it is pervasive
* The total volume of contribution and positioning determines competitive advantage

Furthermore, contribution to open source is also effective from an ROI perspective:

* ROI from contribution: 2–5x ※1
* Maximized when reflected in technology strategy

Therefore, the MVP is not the goal.

> **The MVP is the starting point for scaling.**

※1: Reports on contribution ROI
* European Commission 2021-9-6 https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/news/commission-publishes-study-impact-open-source-european-economy
* European Commission 2021-9-2 https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/library/study-about-impact-open-source-software-and-hardware-technological-independence-competitiveness-and
* Linux Foundation 2026-2 https://www.linuxfoundation.org/research/contribution-roi

---

### ■ Success Criteria

* Creating outcomes that lead to contracts or are reusable
* Giving back externally (PR/Issue)
* Confirming expandability to other teams

---

### ■ An Important Caution

Starting small and ending small are entirely different things.

Once the first circuit succeeds, it must be expanded to the entire organization through:

* Horizontal expansion
* Talent development
* Integration into evaluation systems

Without this, it does not become structure.

Unless this happens, it will not become organizational competitive advantage.

---

## 5.7 Typical Failures and How to Avoid Them

* Strengthening only connection → Does not become value
* Strengthening only implementation → Diverges from external reality
* Strengthening only proposals → Content becomes hollow
* Stopping at small start → Does not become organizational competitive advantage

**Avoidance strategies**:

* Always design balance as a circuit
* Design so it can cycle many times
* Design for scale from the earliest stage

---

### ■ The Limits of a Structure That Doesn't Scale

Even if the conversion circuit functions in part, it means nothing if it doesn't spread to the entire organization.

* Runs only in specific teams
* Depends on specific individuals
* Does not expand horizontally

In this state, outcomes remain locally optimized.

---

### ■ What a Structure That Scales Looks Like

Scale is not mere expansion.

* The same structure is reproduced across multiple teams
* Contribution becomes a "surface" rather than isolated "points"
* The organization's overall density of involvement increases

---

### ■ Conclusion

> **A conversion circuit that doesn't scale does not become competitive advantage.**

A conversion circuit is insufficient if merely designed.

**It only has meaning when it permeates the entire organization.**

---

## 5.8 Conclusion

Transformation does not happen by accident.

> **Transformation is only reproduced through design.**

And once the conversion circuit begins to run, employees contribute more spontaneously.

> **But for that to become competitive advantage, it must scale.**

Connection is the prerequisite. Transformation generates value, which steps up to contribution, which scales into organizational capability — and that is what determines competitive advantage.

The conversion circuit cannot function through individuals alone.

* Someone who connects
* Someone who understands
* Someone who implements
* Someone who proposes

As long as these remain disconnected, the circuit risks breaking.

What is therefore needed is:

> **Organizational design premised on the circuit.**

---

## 6.2 The Limits of Conventional Organizations

Conventional organizations are fragmented as follows:

* Technology division: Development
* Business division: Sales
* Research division: Exploration

Each is optimized, but not connected.

In this state, the conversion circuit cannot function.

---

## 6.3 The Required Organizational Structure

For the conversion circuit to function, the following are required:

* A function for external connection
* A function for internal transformation
* A function for business connection

These must not be fragmented — they must possess a structure that flows.

---

## 6.4 The Concept of a Program Office

One form that realizes this structure is a Program Office.

* OSPO (Open Source Program Office)
* ISPO (Inner Source Program Office)

These initiatives are not merely functions.

**They are structures that support a circuit from its non-functional dimensions.**

---

## 6.5 Connecting Evaluation and Roles

Evaluation is necessary to maintain the structure.

* Connection is evaluated
* Transformation is evaluated
* Contribution is evaluated

Without this, structure cannot be maintained.

> **A structure that is not evaluated will inevitably disappear.**

Evaluation must be designed and shared not only at the individual level but also at:

* The team level
* The organizational level

---

## 6.6 The Conditions for Permeation

Permeating the organization takes time.

But that is not a problem.

What matters is:

> **If the structure is right, it will inevitably permeate.**

---

## 6.7 The Limits of Human Capital Management — And Its Redesign

Human capital management is important.

But it is not sufficient.

Current human capital management practice can be broadly organized into the following areas:

* Visualization and measurement of human resources
* Education and reskilling
* Talent management
* Diversity initiatives
* External collaboration

---

All of these are necessary.

But there is a shared premise underlying their structure.

> **Human resources are treated as subjects to be optimized within the organization.**

Education is internal capability improvement.

Placement is optimization of internal resources.

Evaluation is measurement against internal metrics.

---

In this view, the relationship with the outside is limited to:

* Explaining to investors
* The relationship with the labor market
* Manageable partner collaboration

> **That is, the outside is treated as a subject of evaluation — not as a source of evolution.**

---

But from the perspective of value creation, the outside has a different meaning.

* New problems exist outside
* Technology evolution happens outside
* Knowledge flows in from outside

Therefore what matters is:

> **Connecting to the outside itself.**

Yet in most human capital management:

> **This connection is not designed as structure.**

---

Even where one-off collaborations and event participation exist:

* Continuous involvement
* Connection as a role
* Activity connected to evaluation

are not established.

As a result:

* Knowledge from outside does not accumulate
* Problem-solving capability is not expanded
* Value creation does not follow

---

What is even more important is the question of motivation.

Those at the frontier of value creation:

* Are not moved by evaluation systems
* Are not moved by KPIs

> **They move only when they connect to external problems.**

---

But when external connection is not designed, this motivation does not arise.

As a result:

* Human resources are optimized internally
* External relationships become formalized
* Innovation does not occur

---

This problem is not a matter of individual initiatives.

> **It is a structural problem.**

Therefore what is needed is not:

* How to develop human resources, but
* **Designing a structure in which human resources continue to connect externally**

---

That structure is one in which:

* Connection is permitted
* Connection is evaluated
* Connection is sustained

Without this perspective, human capital management:

> **Cannot transcend the frame of internal optimization.**

---

## 6.8 Conclusion

The organization exists to run the conversion circuit.
And the circuit's input is connection with the outside.

When that circuit scales:

* Human resources function as capital
* The value generated arises naturally
* The organization demonstrates that value within the ecosystem

> **Structure activates human resources, and human resources strengthen structure.**

When this cycle is established:

> **The organization possesses sustainable competitive advantage for the first time.**

* Depend on specific conditions
* Are not reproduced

A structure with sustainability:

Sustainability cannot be achieved through will.

# Chapter 9: Structure Extends to Society

## 9.1 Why Structure Goes Beyond the Organization

The structure described in this book is not confined within organizations.

Open source is, by its very nature, a structure open to the outside.

* Technology evolves outside
* Decisions are made in the community
* Standards are formed within the ecosystem

Therefore:

> **An organization's structure defines how it connects with the outside.**

---

How an organization connects,
how it transforms,
how it contributes —

the results of these determine:

* Position within the ecosystem
* Influence over technology
* The structure of value distribution

---

This structure does not remain within a single organization.

> **It extends, as is, to industry and then to society.**

---

## 9.2 The Meaning of Human Resources in the Ecosystem

At the center of this is "human resources."

But not in the conventional sense.

* Someone knowledgeable about a particular technology
* Someone who can develop

That alone is insufficient.

---

Human resources in open source are:

> **Those who carry out connection, transformation, and contribution within the structure.**

---

Such people cannot be defined by a single skill.

At minimum, they need three axes.

---

### ■ Technical Axis

* OSS understanding
* Implementation capability
* Architectural design

---

### ■ Expansion Axis

* Bridging external and internal (Bridge)
* Communicating the value of technology (Advocate)
* Indicating direction (Leadership)

---

### ■ Temporal Axis

* Tracking technology trends
* Understanding ecosystem changes
* Insight into future consequences

---

Few people possess all three simultaneously.

But without such people:

* Connection becomes shallow
* Transformation does not occur
* Contribution is not reached

---

## 9.3 Human Resources That Are Not Defined

There is an important problem here.

Such people are:

> **Not defined as an institutional category.**

---

Conventional human resource models use classifications such as:

* Developer
* Infrastructure engineer
* Architect

But the roles in open source cannot be expressed by these.

---

Similarly:

* Educational systems
* Career paths
* Qualification frameworks

are not in place.

---

As a result:

* They arise only accidentally
* They are not reproduced
* They do not accumulate in the organization

> This is a structural problem of society.

---

## 9.4 Maintainer Burnout as a Social Loss

A structure that is not evaluated is exhausting people.

---

In open source, maintainers are central figures.

* They fix bugs
* They maintain functionality
* They judge direction

The sustainability of the ecosystem depends on their existence.

---

But this structure is extremely fragile.

In most cases:

* Contribution is unpaid
* Evaluation is not visible
* Responsibility alone increases

What results from this is:

> **Maintainer burnout.**

---

This is not merely an individual problem.

* Excellent maintainers leave
* Project evolution stops
* Security risk increases

In other words:

> **It is a social loss.**

Therefore, it is a problem for which both organizations and society must bear structural responsibility.

---

The essence of this problem is not workload.

> **It is a structural problem.**

As stated in Chapter 6:

> A structure that is not evaluated will inevitably disappear.

Maintainer burnout is a manifestation of this principle.

---

When contribution is not evaluated and not structurally supported,

sustainability cannot be established.

This connects directly to the question of "sustainability" discussed in Chapter 8.

* There is no autonomous sustainability
* There is no propagation through structure

Therefore:

> **It becomes a structure that can only be maintained at the cost of human exhaustion.**

This state will eventually collapse.

---

What matters is not protecting maintainers.

> **It is designing a structure in which maintainers do not burn out.**

* Contribution is evaluated
* Roles are clear
* Continuity is supported by structure

When this state is established,
sustainability becomes real for the first time.

---

Conversely:

> **As long as maintainer burnout occurs, that structure is incomplete,**
> **and maintainer burnout is an indicator of structural defects.**

Without addressing this, open source will not sustain.

And that is:

> **Manifesting as a loss for society as a whole.**

---

## 9.5 The Need for an Open Source Human Resource Model

What is therefore needed is:

> **The definition and modeling of open source human resources.**

---

This is not merely skill definition.

It must include:

* Roles within the structure
* Relationship with the ecosystem
* Capability including the temporal dimension

---

Useful reference frameworks here are those in Japan such as:

* ITSS (IT Skill Standards)
* DSS (Digital Skill Standards)

---

However, these primarily address:

* Technical skills
* Business skills

> **What is needed for open source is an extension of these.**

---

## 9.6 A Structural Definition of OSS Human Resources (Draft)

This book defines open source human resources as follows:

> **Open source human resources are those who function within a structure that connects to the external ecosystem, transforms its value internally, and returns it externally.**

---

Their capabilities are organized along three axes.

| Axis | Content |
|------|---------|
| Technical | OSS understanding, implementation capability, design capability |
| Expansion | Bridging, advocacy, leadership |
| Temporal | Tracking trends, structural understanding, insight |

---

These three axes are not independent.

* Without technical capability, trust cannot be earned
* Without expansion, value cannot be realized
* Without the temporal axis, direction is lost

Therefore what matters is:

> **Integrating and operationalizing all three axes.**

---

## 9.7 The Absence of Education and Institutional Frameworks

Currently, there is no systematic mechanism for developing such human resources.

* Not covered in university education
* Fragmented in corporate education
* Not defined as qualifications

This is not accidental.

It is because conventional models cannot accommodate these human resources.

---

But this state cannot be maintained going forward.

Because:

> **Whether one can participate in the ecosystem determines competitive advantage.**

---

## 9.8 Impact on Society

In a society where such human resources are scarce:

* Technology depends on external sources
* Organizations only follow standards
* The source of value is lost

---

In a society where such human resources exist as structure:

* Organizational can participate in the direction of technology
* Can influence the ecosystem
* Can participate in the flow of knowledge

---

This difference connects directly to:

* Industrial competitiveness
* Digital sovereignty

And:

> **Once this gap forms, it widens over time and cannot easily be reversed.**

---

## 9.9 Conclusion

Open source is not confined to the problem of a single organization.

It is not merely a human resources problem.

> **It is also a problem of societal structure.**

---

But that structure is:

* Designed by organizations
* Carried by human resources
* Extended to society

---

Therefore:

> **The definition and propagation of open source human resources is a social challenge.**

---

Having structure is only the starting point for organizations.

When that structure spreads to society,

only then:

> **Is the foundation for sustainably generating value within the ecosystem established.**
